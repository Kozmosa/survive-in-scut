# The Science and Technology of Self-Study: An Anti-Involution Guide to University Survival Engineering

## Zero: Going to School vs. Learning

### The Brutal Reality

The *Shanghai Jiao Tong University Survival Guide* opens with a naked exposé of the state of Chinese higher education:

"The undergraduate teaching at the vast majority of domestic universities is not on the verge of collapse — it collapsed long ago."

Eighteen years later, this sentence still holds true.

The school's goal is to secure research funding and build up xx disciplines. That's how the school stays alive. As for undergraduates — whether you learn anything or not is none of their concern when they're chasing grants. Why would they care if you sink or swim? Even if they did have to care, managing master's and PhD students delivers far better ROI.

And for university faculty, teaching is merely a "side gig" — or even just a "chore." Their primary energy goes into research. Only by doing well in research can they secure funding and climb the academic ranks. Teaching, especially teaching undergrads, is a thankless grind. Do it well and there's no reward; do it poorly and there's punishment.

Don't assume lecturers — those with lighter research burdens — will actually put effort into teaching either. Teach seriously and the paycheck is the same. Phone it in and the paycheck is the same. Why wouldn't they slack off? Whether students actually understand? What's that got to do with them?

From PowerPoint slides older than we are to mind-numbing lectures, from cookie-cutter exams to course content recycled from a decade ago — this sentence has echoed across 18 years and can still be corroborated everywhere you look.

So if you ever encounter a teacher who lectures with patience and care, shares the cutting edge of their field, and tells you what the industry is actually like — treasure them. They're a rare resource in this institution.

Now let's talk about the curriculum. Here's a shocking conclusion: university curricula lag behind industry by at least 5 years. Falling behind by 10 or even 20 years is hardly unusual.

Take big data as an example. Big data began drawing attention from top-tier internet companies around 2008. By 2013 it was gaining widespread attention from government, capital, and media. In 2014 it was first written into the *Government Work Report*. By 2016 it was exploding into practical applications across major sectors. In 2021, laws like the *Data Security Law* were introduced and regulation tightened. Today, big data is no longer an unfamiliar term to any of us.

Yet the corresponding major — Data Science and Big Data Technology — was only approved by the Ministry of Education in 2016, with Peking University, Central South University, and others among the first to offer it. The earliest undergraduates from this program graduated in 2020 at best. Postgraduates? Not until 2023.

Students in this major perfectly missed the industry's golden window.

And that's a *new* field. For disciplines with centuries of accumulated scientific and technological heritage, the lag is even more severe. Much of the content is still stuck in the early 2000s. The teachers themselves have never touched the industry frontier — and even when they have, it rarely goes beyond inviting some "big names" to give a talk on campus.

I once screenshotted a passage from a lecture slide and sent it to an industry heavyweight. His first response was that half of what this teacher was saying was flat-out wrong — there's no way this person had ever set foot on the front lines.

If the teachers themselves don't know what's happening in the industry, how could they possibly avoid churning out an obsolete curriculum?

And then there's the problem of scale: several hundred students in a single major, all sharing the same curriculum. Everyone's thinking style, knowledge background, and learning capacity are different. How can you possibly guarantee that applying a one-size-fits-all curriculum works effectively for every individual?

It's like cooking a giant wok of cafeteria slop — some parts come out half-raw, others burnt to a crisp. You simply can't stir it evenly. Teachers can only optimize for the group average; individual optimization is out of the question.

This brings us to something that has to be mentioned: "innovation classes." Every 985 university loves running these — names like "Excellence Class," "Academician Class," and some even set up an entire dedicated college just to house all the "innovation classes" across the university.

When these programs are advertised, they come loaded with labels like "bachelor-master-PhD integrated track," "mentorship system," "small-class teaching." The policy favoritism may not be entirely false, but weighed against the dark side lurking beneath, these so-called advantages — while they might not be entirely meaningless — are at best negligible.

Beneath the glossy exterior of these programs lie several massive pitfalls:

**1. GPA Deflation**

Inside an innovation class, the workload is double that of a regular class, and the exam difficulty is triple — yet the GPA scale is still capped at 4.0, same as everyone else. When applying for graduate school or studying abroad, external professors and foreign admissions officers don't care whether you took "Advanced Quantum Mechanics (English Instruction)" or General Physics. They only look at the number. (Your own university is the exception.) That 3.6 you clawed out on nightmare difficulty? In the application market, it gets beaten by the 3.8 that someone in a regular class picked up without breaking a sweat. This is a transaction where the input and the return are wildly mismatched.

**2. A Rat Race Colosseum**

Innovation classes, especially those in popular majors, gather the most obsessive grinders from every corner of the country. In a regular class you might reach the upper-middle ranks without too much pressure, but here you can only serve as background decoration for the grind gods — quietly doing your duty as a denominator.

Last semester I chatted with a grinder who had transferred into a top-tier class in a popular major. They told me that those grind gods all have course averages of 92+, national scholarships drop into their laps like spare change, and their competition certificates are thicker than a dictionary. They don't even let volunteering and extracurricular activities slip through their fingers.

"In our class, people even fight to sign up for sports meet events — because it counts toward conduct points." They grind even at the margins where the returns are infinitesimal.

So if you're not doing well in this kind of class, it's not that you're inadequate — it's that you've been placed in the wrong coordinate system.

**3. Cannon Fodder for the Research Pipeline**

The school's goal isn't to turn you into an outstanding engineer or an industry leader. It's to lock you onto the "in-house PhD student" track and turn you into cheap research labor for your professors. The moment you realize partway through that you're not cut out for research, you'll discover that the dragon-slaying techniques you've learned are utterly useless in the job market.

Research, too, is a brutal game of "behind one general's glory lie ten thousand bleached bones." Even if you're at the very top of your class, there's no guarantee you'll be that "one general."

They promise to train you into a scientist. In reality, they're training you into disposable material on the research assembly line. The school doesn't care whether you live or die — it only cares about publishing papers and producing results. As for who produces those results? Well, if you won't do it, there are plenty who will.

**4. No Exit Mechanism**

Many "innovation classes" enforce a bottom-rank elimination system but have virtually no mechanism for voluntary withdrawal. Even if you're not at the very bottom of the barrel, trying to exit or transfer into a regular class means facing massive administrative pressure and psychological shame. Those who quit are branded as failures; those who don't quit can only serve as denominators for the grind gods. So the people who were already unhappy with their studies sink deeper and deeper into depression under this double pressure. One path blocked at both ends.

### The Diminishing Returns of GPA Grinding

GPA is cash, not an asset.

As your GPA climbs higher and higher, you'll find that grinding for it delivers worse and worse returns.

The grind gods in popular majors at good schools all have averages above 90. Let's say your average is 80. The time and energy you spend maintaining your average versus what a 90+ grinder spends maintaining theirs — these two things aren't even in the same universe.

The only uses of GPA are for graduate school recommendations and studying abroad. But graduate school recommendations look at your GPA *rank*, not the GPA number itself. And for studying abroad, you can endlessly retake courses to "brush up" your GPA.

Different score bands are qualitatively different:

- **60–80**: This is the "fundamentals zone." As long as you grasp the basic concepts and memorize the formulas, you won't fail. With a bit of luck you can even get a decent-looking score.
- **80–90**: This is the "improvement zone." Here you need to really understand the key and tricky points of the exams. Time invested starts creeping upward.
- **90–100**: This is the "bubble zone." For a one- or two-point improvement, you might have to memorize the entire textbook, or even try to read the exam-setter's mind.

Going from 65 to 70 might cost you 5 extra hours. Going from 90 to 95? Fifty hours might not even be enough. And once those 5 points get weighted into your GPA, the effect might amount to nothing more than a 0.2 difference.

Even more insane: for those fractional points, people will even fight over things as marginal as becoming the class rep for a blow-off course. To deny someone else that sliver of a point, they'd rather spend ten times the time and effort.

That 0.2 points might matter if you're on the borderline for graduate school recommendation. But in a graduate school interview or a postgraduate entrance exam retest, its return is exactly zero. Professors don't care how a 95 is better than a 90. But they can tell at a glance whether you've "done a complete project" or you're "just a problem-solving machine."

If someone treats the policy scoring rubric as holy scripture, then they've become the policy's biggest victim. Yet the reality is: in popular majors, people fight tooth and nail in the "bubble zone" just to avoid falling behind. Even the "winners" of this system can't escape being sacrificed.

After all, exams are a game we know well. The rules are clear, and it's comfortable and safe. Just keep playing the same way you did in high school. Everyone's packed onto the same narrow bridge — it would be weirder if they *weren't* grinding themselves to death.

The rational strategy is "cutting your losses": once you reach your target line, stop investing extra effort. Just aim to "hold steady" so you don't drop out of the safe zone.

How to hold steady — that's what comes later.

### Is Attending Class the Same as Learning?

Going to school and learning are two different concepts.

The learning path we've followed since childhood is: "preview → attend class → take notes → do homework → take exams." On this path, we're in a passive receiving position — there's no active construction.

From elementary school to middle school to high school, and then to university, we've been repeating this same path. For the first 18 years we had no choice. But now we have the internet, we have AI — a vast sea of resources waiting to be tapped.

After reading what's come before, I trust you already believe that university teachers have no intention of teaching seriously. We absolutely can choose to self-study.

But here's the thing: what many people call "self-study" is nothing more than finding online courses and re-watching, online, the lectures they couldn't understand offline. They still haven't broken out of the passive reception path.

Why are we so addicted to this illusion? Because it delivers a lethal dopamine hit.

Whether you're in a physical classroom or watching a recorded lecture, your train of thought follows the teacher's logic. The derivations flow seamlessly. The example problems seem doable. Everything feels great.

In reality, you're just being "carried" by the teacher. These paths are ones the teacher walked you through — you haven't actually walked them yourself. You *feel* like you understood, but it's really just the comfortable sensation of a brain massage.

Try reciting what you just learned, or solving a problem — you'll freeze instantly. Because you never truly mastered that logic.

Input only, no output. Understand everything in class, can't do a single problem on your own. Using class attendance to escape output is nothing more than a feeble form of avoidance.

Real "learning" is painful — full of chaos and uncertainty, like working out, where your muscles have to tear before they can regrow. Attending class, on the other hand, is comfortable — full of certainty.

Most people would rather read a textbook ten times than derive a single formula themselves. They'd rather re-watch an online course five times than independently solve one problem.

And this inertia — "chasing certainty," "escaping pain," "performative diligence" — is precisely the trap that the "schooling" system has meticulously designed for you.

Schooling is a **finite game**. The goal is to beat everyone else, or to satisfy a checklist of metrics. Everyone's grinding, and you get swept along to grind too. It's kill or be killed, with a single evaluation system, and the game ends when the results come out.

Learning is an **infinite game**. The goal is to build understanding and solve problems. It's driven by curiosity and passion, with diverse and immediate evaluation systems. The greatest purpose of this game is to keep the game going.

So, "going to school" is merely a job we have to do. "Learning" is the endeavor truly worth investing in.

Yet what we face is a zero-sum situation: the school not only refuses to teach you anything useful, it also tries to occupy every minute of your time so you have none left for real learning.

Just look around campus: meaningless activities, performative group assignments, insipid team-building exercises. They're all looting your attention, keeping you perpetually exhausted and unable to settle down for active construction.

So for the sake of learning, we must rebel against the school's existing model.

### De-Studentification

As the last stop before entering society, what university should be about is "de-studentification."

My department head once shared an insight: the time undergraduates spend on coursework should decrease with each year. The time freed up should go toward "things you can put on your résumé."

Graduating seniors I've interviewed told me that what you learn in university covers less than one-tenth of what the job demands. And this is for engineering majors who actually find relevant work.

HR sorts graduates into three tiers: those with substantial project experience, those with strong overall skills but higher training costs, and those with nothing to show beyond their GPA and student cadre titles.

You graduate at 22. If the only new things you can put on your résumé are course grades and nothing else, then those four years are a blank.

So what *can* go on a résumé? How should you allocate those four years?

- **Year 1**: Everyone's a rookie at this stage. There's not much you can do beyond coursework. Put 80% of your time into courses like Advanced Mathematics, University Physics, and so on — push those foundational course grades as high as you can. Courses like Advanced Math, Physics, and Probability Theory carry huge credit weight. Nailing them gives you a massive GPA advantage. Here's a brutal truth: later specialized courses don't carry that much credit. Your Year 1 GPA ranking is pretty much your ranking for the entire four years. So the first year requires some hard work on coursework — it buys you margin for error over the next two years.

- **Year 2**: Time spent on coursework should be under 60% of the total class hours for required exam courses. After a year of fumbling your way through campus life, you've got some knowledge under your belt, you've figured out some exam hacks, and you've built up some information channels. The focus now is *exploration*. Reach out to professors and join a research group. Form teams for competitions. Get involved in clubs. Learn skills. Or, like me, write a practical PDF guide. Whatever you do, there must be output — achievements beyond coursework, even if the achievements themselves aren't that impressive.

- **Year 3**: This is the year to lock in your direction. Employment, postgraduate entrance exams, graduate school recommendation, studying abroad — even starting a business. Every path lies before you, and once you pick one, you have to go all in. If employment, find internships boldly. If postgraduate exams, camp out in the library and grind practice problems. If further study, go all-out on competitions and publishing papers. If entrepreneurship, run your project like a maniac. Don't delude yourself into thinking you can shine on multiple fronts — most people struggle just to walk a single path well. By this point the courses are mostly whatever, with maybe only one high-credit course left. If you're still too scared to skip class for fear of losing attendance points, or still staying up late chasing perfect homework scores — like a Year 1 student — then you're a "strategic infant."

- **Year 4**: By now everyone's going their separate ways. Courses are practically nonexistent. Every person drifts further down their own path, arriving at their own results. Not much else to say.

A pretty GPA with nothing else that stands out — it looks perfect, but it's incredibly fragile. When every single person competing alongside you is holding a transcript full of 90+ averages, how exactly do you plan to stand out?

Coursework is your "cash flow" — just keep it above the safety line. Projects and competitions are your "assets" — they determine your future value. As you advance through the years, you need to aggressively convert cash flow into assets, rather than sitting on a pile of depreciating currency (GPA) and patting yourself on the back.

### Beyond Review

In university, learning well and testing well have absolutely nothing to do with each other.

Since schooling is merely a job we have to do, our mindset should shift: use the shortest time and the least energy to exchange for your target score.

I tallied it up: this semester, I spent roughly 45 hours on "schooling." Meanwhile, the total class hours for required exam courses came to 162. Using less than one-third of the time, I still managed 80+ in every course and held onto the #1 GPA rank.

So everything that follows is built on one assumption: you do not plan to attend class. You've decided to self-study the whole way. You want to free up that two-thirds of your time to do the things you actually want to do — no matter how heretical those things may look to the outside world.

You don't need to learn everything the way you did in high school.

University final exams are not like high school. High school exams are selection-based; university exams are qualification-based. University exams aren't designed to filter out geniuses — they're designed to verify合格品 (qualified products). Our goal is not the perfect 100. Our goal is the most cost-effective 85+ or just passing.

So from the very beginning, don't even think about tackling exams the way you did in high school — grinding problem sets like a maniac. In university, you have time, you have choice, and you have unrestricted access to the internet and AI. Use every tool at your disposal. Maximize efficiency. That is the game of university.

Many courses were designed from the ground up to be crammable.

Don't believe me? Go search the course name on Bilibili with "crash course." You'll find speedrun courses for the vast majority of general education courses and quite a few specialized ones. But have you ever wondered — despite all the teachers stressing "study hard all semester long" — why these crash courses still rack up millions of views? Is it simply because most students don't study during the semester?

At the very least, these courses prove one thing: learning an entire course in a few hours is completely doable.

You might resent how teachers coast through their lectures and hate the baffling assignments, only to realize right before finals that you've learned nothing. But don't panic. High school exams are a marathon; university is interval sprints.

High school requires steady, incremental study all semester. University requires yanking your memory to its peak right before the exam.

High school knowledge points are simple, but question types are varied with lots of tricks — that's what makes them feel "hard." University, on the other hand, has a massive volume of knowledge points but simple question types. You're tested on exactly what was taught, with few variations. Professors generally won't throw you curveballs or set traps.

For professors, failing students just creates headaches for them too. So most teachers will try as hard as they can to give you points. Even if you mess up your post-exam score estimate, don't worry too much — your actual score is often higher than you predicted.

So in university, the core competency isn't "how to learn well." It's "how to test well."

## Part One: The Day-to-Day Game

Burying your head in books is tactical diligence and strategic laziness.

Take a look around — the high-GPA people near you roughly fall into two categories: those who grind nonstop from day one to finals, and those who are just really damn good at exams.

Which one do you pick?

I'm not saying grinding is wrong — I'm saying there's a more efficient way. And honestly, if you were the grinding type, you probably wouldn't have made it this far into this document.

### "Skipping Class"

There's no positive correlation between attendance and grades. If anything, it's negative.

By now, it's an unspoken truth that professors don't actually teach seriously. But what are *you* doing in class?

Listening intently? You'll learn nothing. If you've actually absorbed the earlier sections of this essay, that thought shouldn't even cross your mind.

Scrolling on your phone, slacking off? Look around. Most of your classmates have their heads down, staring at glowing screens. Scrolling TikTok, playing Honor of Kings, doing whatever. They're just being driven by the primal instinct of "I don't want to listen" — with zero concept of *why* they're not listening.

Bodies in jail, minds wandering.

Doing your own thing? Sounds better. But what exactly? Homework? Watching online courses? Group project slides? Your own side project?

If you're certain that the knowledge density of a lecture is lower than the knowledge density of reading on your own, then skip class without hesitation. Or "stealth-skip": physically present, mentally doing your own thing. Body checked in, soul checked out.

I'm a strong advocate of skipping class. And as class monitor, I've driven the cost of "authorized skipping" — taking leave — down to near zero. Write yourself a leave slip, give me a heads-up, no signatures needed. Done.

There are so many things more interesting than sitting in class.

As the saying goes: if you're gonna skip, don't be scared. If you're scared, don't skip. Skipping class is the art of risk management — minimize the participation-grade penalty while pulling off the physical escape.

Figure out the professor's roll-call patterns. Have your roommate answer for you. Sign in for each other. When there's a policy from above, there's a workaround from below. If you want to skip class, there's no class you can't skip.

Only go to class when necessary. Even when you go, decisively do your own thing. The classroom is our "miscellaneous errand center."

What about roll calls?

If you physically skip: treat your roommate to a meal in advance, give them a little benefit, clearly state what you need, ask them properly. Food seals mouths, gifts soften hands.

If you mentally skip: just saying "I don't know" isn't the end of the world. You feel embarrassed in the moment, but most people will forget your embarrassing scene right after class. If you're really scared, just tune out during roll call and generate an answer with AI.

Even if you actually get marked absent — it's just the price of 90 minutes of freedom. When the payoff of a lecture is far below its opportunity cost, *staying* is the real waste.

Skipping class isn't a "bad student" label. It's an investment. Getting docked on participation is just the transaction's penalty fee.

Let me share a trick to dodge random roll calls on Rain Classroom:

Scan the QR code to check in normally. But during the roll-call window, disconnect all your devices from the internet. Log out of WeChat on your computer — the key is to make WeChat go offline. Then you simply won't get picked. This works brilliantly for large lectures, especially political courses.

As for how to catch up on what you missed, we'll get into that in detail later.

### Impression Management

Most professors do grade-lift borderline cases — but don't take it for granted.

Put yourself in the professor's shoes. Facing a student you have zero impression of, who never does homework, and whose exam scores are a disaster — would *you* feel like lifting them?

Professors are human. Students they remember — especially those they remember fondly — naturally get higher participation grades. Their final exam grading might even be looser.

Even if participation doesn't count for much, it can decide whether your final score is 59 or 60, whether your GPA is 3.7 or 4.0.

And compared to grinding like hell during finals week, participation points are laughably easy to earn. Don't look down on those few points. Your grade is built point by point — it's not deducted point by point.

The fine print on participation grading may leave little wiggle room, but rules are dead and people are alive. Making a good impression on your professor never hurts, and it might pay off when you least expect it.

You don't need to pay attention every single class. But in key sessions — like the first or last lecture — aggressively assert your presence.

"Class monitor" and "academic committee member" are two roles that make it easy to build presence. Approaching the professor in these roles — they have no reason to turn you away. Many professors can probably only name the students in these two roles anyway.

No official title? No problem. Use AI beforehand to prepare a question that can only be asked if you've actually previewed the material. Go up to the professor after class and ask it. Make them think you're "diligent." Make them remember your face and your name.

It's fine. We're asking questions purely for the sake of asking questions.

Missed that chance? There's still a method. Whenever you do go to class, sit in the front row. Even if you're on your laptop doing something else entirely, the professor will default to "front-row student = good student."

The emotional value of sitting in the front row for a professor far exceeds that of sleeping in the back row.

In my second semester of freshman year, for high-credit courses like calculus, I sat in the front row every single class. Those professors all knew my name. They even frequently called me up to the board to solve problems.

### Study Rhythm

Why is a university class about 90 minutes (45×2)?

Because it's convenient for the academic affairs office to schedule. It lets you only haul yourself to the lecture building 1–2 times a week. That's it.

The university timetable was never designed for your brain's optimal absorption of knowledge. It's just a compromise around professors' free time. But the human attention span maxes out at about 40 minutes. Designing class periods right at that limit is like setting off on a long road trip with exactly enough gas — zero margin for error. The slightest detour and you're stranded. If you can go through an entire lecture without slacking off even once, you probably wouldn't be here.

Now factor in forgetting. When you show up next Tuesday for Thursday's lecture, you've already forgotten every single thing from the previous session. The professor has to spend the first 15 minutes reviewing for you, then cram new material down your throat for the remaining time.

This cycle repeats for over a dozen weeks. Every session is writing data onto a "formatted" brain. Brutally inefficient.

Similarly, different subject types call for different study rhythms. Memorization-heavy content thrives on short, high-frequency stimulation. Logical-mathematical content needs a large uninterrupted block of immersion.

The university doesn't distinguish between these two types. It stuffs every course indiscriminately into the 45×2 timetable — and expects us to learn something.

That in itself is absurd enough.

Lectures are just "brain massages." The professor pretends to explain clearly, the students pretend to understand, and everyone feels comfortable.

But without forced overload, there is no substantive reinforcement. Beneficial things are uncomfortable. Grinding through formulas, memorizing statutes, debugging code — these tasks come with inherent discomfort. And it's precisely that discomfort that lights up new neural connections.

For memorization-heavy subjects — like every political course — our operating logic is "small meals, many times."

Put in about one hour per day, but don't do it all at once. Split it into three chunks of 20 minutes each, dedicated purely to memorizing. Repeated, high-frequency stimulation of the relevant neurons.

This is relatively easy, won't crush you with "quota" pressure, and when you memorize during peak focus, your brain absorbs the most — harder to forget.

One session a week, seven hours of vocabulary in one go — that's self-deception. Your brain stops accepting input after the first hour. Forty-five minutes a day, three times a day, making neurons fire repeatedly — that's playing by biology's rules.

For knowledge that's highly procedural and structural — like advanced math, mechanics, coding — that's when we need the "immersive experience."

In these cases, any fragmented interruption breaks our continuity. Even just getting up to use the bathroom might make you forget the train of thought you just had.

If you're deriving a complex mechanics formula or configuring a machine learning environment, absolutely do not use the so-called "Pomodoro Technique" to interrupt yourself. The cold-start cost for this type of knowledge is extremely high. It's like boiling water — if you turn off the flame every few minutes, the water will never boil.

In moments like these, what you need is to go to the library, put down your phone, give yourself an absolute three-hour immersion block, and enter a flow state.

If you happen to have a free afternoon and want to learn something to make finals easier, don't just grind one subject to death. Use "interleaved learning":

Start with about an hour and a half of high-intensity computation (like working through college physics example problems), then rest for 10 minutes. During this break, stand up, move around, use the bathroom — but absolutely do not touch your phone.

The moment you open your phone, dopamine levels in your brain spike. It becomes incredibly hard to return to a focused state. And by the time you put your phone down ready to continue, an hour has probably already slipped by.

Next, spend an hour on memorization-based content — cram some political theory, do a couple of English reading exercises. At this point, you're activating the brain's "language" regions while the "logic" regions rest.

After another 10-minute break, finish with organizing and output. By switching between different subject types this way, mental fatigue drops dramatically.

### Information Channels

Final exams aren't just a test of study ability — they're a test of information channels.

What information do you specifically need?

1. **Textbooks**: All knowledge and exam content originates from the textbook. But don't read it word for word. Treat the textbook like a "dictionary" — flip to it when something's unclear.
2. **Lecture slides**: Slides are the condensed version of the textbook. They consolidate all potentially testable knowledge points in one place. Your professor's slides might be older than you are, but that doesn't stop you from studying them carefully.
3. **Notes and exam experience from seniors**: If you know seniors with great notes, do everything you can to get them. Probe them about exam difficulty and review strategies. After getting the goods, send a small red packet — don't just say "thanks." Whether they accept it is their business; you need to show the attitude that you're not a freeloader.
4. **Past exam papers**: The absolute top priority. The vast majority of university final exams haven't changed in a decade, especially in STEM. The professor is highly likely to take past exam questions, tweak a few numbers, or wrap them in a different scenario. Even if the question types shift somewhat, you'll still know roughly which topics get tested and at what difficulty level. If possible, get at least 3 sets.
5. **Key-point review sessions**: Some professors will dedicate an entire class near finals to highlighting key points. Even if you've tuned out all semester, *this* class you must seize. Listen with full attention. Identify what's key, what won't be tested, what shows up as short questions, what shows up as long questions. If anything's still unclear, chase the professor down after class. Ideally, keep asking until they get annoyed.

With this information, your exam prep foundation is basically set. If you're missing some of it, don't worry — we still have AI. Due to space constraints, I won't go into detail here. We'll cover how to use AI specifically in a future update.

None of this means attending lectures doesn't matter. If you're aiming for graduate school recommendation / studying abroad, or just don't want finals week to crush you, listening attentively during the semester is still very much worth it.

What kinds of courses are worth listening to?

- **High-credit courses**: Generally, courses with 3 or more credits are fairly tough — like calculus, linear algebra, college physics. Check your school's department website for the exact credit breakdown in your program's training plan.
- **Courses with high view counts on crash-course videos**: At the start of the semester, go look up crash courses online. The view count will give you a sense of how tough the course is. If it's high, you'll probably need to go through it multiple times to grasp it.

Even though I choose the speedrun path, I do study seriously for certain key courses. There's another benefit to studying seriously: the professor remembers you well, and your participation grade might go higher. Everyone knows participation can pull a 59 to a 60 — but what if it can also pull an 89 to a 90?

The 90+ formula: study seriously during the semester + high-efficiency last-minute cramming + high-quality information channels.

### Distributed Computing

Those 90+ average GPA grinders are your external processing units.

The zero-sum game of the Gaokao forced us into solo combat — cutthroat, every man for himself. But in university, there's room for cooperation.

Especially those above-average students — the ones who pay attention in every class, take meticulous notes, and do their homework diligently. They are your best resource channels.

Outsiders might see them as try-hards, but in reality, most excellent people only care about themselves. Their focus is "I want to become better," not "I need to crush so-and-so."

So if you can step up and volunteer — tell them "I won't be dead weight," or even "I can trade you something in return" — they'll usually welcome it.

Use your strengths — information asymmetry, AI tool proficiency, or simply treating them to a meal — to trade for their notes and homework. I said *trade*. Trade. Fucking *trade*.

Don't just waltz up and go "hey bro/sis, can I borrow your notes?" Nobody likes being freeloaded off. They might not say no, but one-way value output never lasts. Value exchange is the only stable model.

And don't complain to them about "this course is so hard" or "I don't understand anything at all" (unless it's a deliberate tactic). Nobody likes someone who spreads negativity but does nothing about it.

After you get the materials? Scan and photocopy the notes. Copy the homework directly. When you hit something you don't understand while copying, use AI to explain it. In university, copying homework is actually a way to boost efficiency.

A senior who ranked #1 in the major once admitted to me: he copies homework all the time too, especially for "memorization courses" — it saves enormous amounts of time.

So where's the "external computing unit" for top-tier students?

Beyond individuals, study groups are also a highly efficient approach. The reason group projects are so inefficient is that nobody steps up to lead, allocate tasks, and drive progress.

So *you* have to be the one who organizes. *You* have to push everyone forward.

Three to four weeks before exams, gather those above-average, average, and just-want-to-pass students. Don't invite the grinders — they probably have their own review plans and don't want to be interrupted.

Then, for the tough courses — like analog electronics, theoretical mechanics — everyone studies one chapter. Two days later, regroup and teach each other. Solve each other's confusion. The act of teaching is itself a form of learning.

This way, each person learns 8 chapters' worth of content while only studying 1 chapter. An 8x efficiency leverage. Far more powerful than going solo.

This is distributed computing. Others handle the front-end data collection. You handle the back-end blitz.

## 2. Subject Triage: Know Your Opponent

Schools don't just standardize class hours — they standardize revision methods too.

Regardless of major, regardless of subject, the revision method drilled into us is "read the textbook — grind problems — memorize answers." Advanced math? That's the method. Ideological and political theory? Same method.

The biggest problem with this model is how crude it is. It assumes every type of subject can be handled the same way, but reality is clearly not like that.

Subjects differ by type. One person can score 95+ in advanced math with ease but barely scrape a pass in political theory. Another can effortlessly memorize regulations and clauses but mentally freeze the moment numbers appear.

### Three Types of Subjects

Different subject types call for different revision methods. But before choosing a revision method, we need to classify our subjects:

1. **Math-Logic Type**
   Primarily tests formulas, reasoning, and calculation. The obvious hallmark is heavy computation on the exam, a single correct answer, and high weight on step-by-step working. Classic examples: calculus, linear algebra, mechanics, circuit theory.

2. **Rote-Memorization Type**
   Primarily tests recall and understanding of key concepts, standards, and regulations. The obvious hallmark is terminology definitions, short-answer questions, and essay questions — open-ended answers but with standard scoring points. Classic examples: ideological and political theory courses, law.

3. **Hybrid Type**
   Exhibits characteristics of both types above. The obvious hallmark is terminology definitions, short-answer questions, and calculation problems all appearing on the same exam, with the same concept appearing in different question types in different ways. Classic examples: economics, and most specialized courses.

This step is crucial — especially for hybrid subjects. Only once you've distinguished the types can you avoid revising aimlessly like a headless chicken.

The subject is mixed, but question types are discrete. It's rare to find a single question that tests both your memory of a concept and your calculation ability.

If you can get past exam papers, or if the instructor has told you the question formats, the first thing you should do is assess the proportion of each question type.

Take one of my own specialized courses as an example: it was 60% memorization + 40% math-logic. A few specific chapters tested both concepts and calculations, so the revision rhythm I chose was: three hours in the morning tackling calculation problems, then three 30-minute sessions after dinner for memorizing concepts.

### A Trap

For hybrid subjects, a single concept may very well be tested in more than one way.

Terminology definitions test your recall of the concept. Short-answer questions test your understanding of the concept's application scenarios. Calculation problems test how you apply the concept within a formula.

You might be fluent at plugging the same concept into a formula in a calculation problem, yet go completely blank when you see it in a short-answer question.

And the most awkward moment: you remembered most of the testing scenarios for a concept, but forgot one — and that exact one shows up on the final exam.

So for concepts in this kind of subject, do a rapid pass before the exam: what is this concept, what does it look like in a short-answer question, and what does it look like in a calculation problem.

## 3. Concepts: Lossless Compression of Knowledge

### First Principles

The mediocre student memorizes 100 question patterns. The smart student masters 1 concept.

High school doesn't place much weight on concepts — roughly go over them, then dive straight into drills. This actually completely violates the fundamental logic of learning. Whether you're learning knowledge or skills, concepts are always the deepest, most foundational layer. All upper-level content is built around concepts.

This is especially true for STEM. Every single problem is an unfolding of fundamental concepts and laws under different boundary conditions.

For STEM subjects, derivations are just as important as concepts. The logical chain shown in a derivation reveals where a formula comes from — understanding that logic is far more efficient than brute-force memorization of formulas.

Within the derivation process, understanding the logical chain matters more than the computation itself. Computation is merely a mechanical process, whereas understanding requires deep thinking.

Most people learn concepts the "language-arts way": rote memorization of definitions. But STEM concept learning should be "parameter-based": understand the meaning of every parameter.

Take the Taylor series, for example. It doesn't much matter if you can't work out the coefficient for each term. The key is understanding that this formula uses addition and multiplication to approximate a function, that the approximation process is continuous differentiation, and the reasoning behind determining the remainder term.

To test your understanding of concepts and formulas, test your understanding the way you'd test code:

1. **Zero/Infinity Test**: What happens if a certain parameter goes to zero / tends toward infinity?
2. **Reversal Test**: If you reverse cause and effect, does the theorem still hold?
3. **Extreme Test**: Under what circumstances does this formula **absolutely not apply**?

If you can't answer the above questions, you can ask an AI directly for assisted understanding.

For subjects leaning toward the rote-memorization type, on the other hand, we can approach memorization and understanding like this:

1. **Text Dimensionality Reduction**: Take a definition or passage, strip away all adjectives and modifiers, and retain only 3–5 scoring-point keywords.
2. **Semantic Substitution**: Imagine you have to explain this concept clearly to a kindergartener — use everyday vocabulary you're familiar with to paraphrase those keywords and jargon.

Back to revision: at the start, you can flip through the book following the table of contents, or have an AI produce a concept checklist for you (not the same as a knowledge-point checklist). First make sure your understanding is sound, then memorize as much as possible.

If you have enough time, this step should take up the lion's share. Only when the foundation is solid can you slowly build the skyscraper.

## 3. Internalization: Reverse-Engineering the Exam Paper

### Reverse Engineering

If the point of doing homework is just to arrive at the correct answer, why not just hand it off to problem-search apps and AI?

The traditional drilling path is "read the question — solve — check the answer — correct." While problem-solving is a good way to test mastery of knowledge, in university exams, how well you've learned and how well you score are unrelated.

Stopping at merely completing the assignment achieves nothing beyond building a little familiarity — it's just linear, low-efficiency busywork. No matter how complex the problem, this is still just a mechanical process.

I trust that by now, you definitely don't want to choose the traditional "sea of drills" approach — you want efficient internalization and mastery.

So what we need to do is reverse-engineer the exam-setter's intent from the answer.

### The Answer-Backwards Method

**1. Problem Selection**

Internalization starts with worked examples and assignments.

From a revision perspective, worked examples and assignments are moderately difficult — they test straightforward application of knowledge points, so they won't crush you with difficulty right out of the gate.

The worked examples on slides and the assignments are actually well worth your attention. Instructors are busy with research and don't have time to write exam papers. Many final exam questions are very likely just examples or exercises with the numbers changed, or even directly copied from textbook originals. Past final exam questions can also show up here.

**2. Look at the Answer First**

Don't attempt it yourself first — look at the answer first. Examine three things: answer structure, scoring points, and disciplinary terminology. Identify what you don't understand first, then work backward from the final result. Where did this number come from? It was obtained from the previous step's data. Then why was this formula used in the previous step? Because the problem statement gave this condition.

Trace back step by step, all the way to the problem's original conditions, and then to the knowledge point being tested. Once you do this, you'll realize that a problem is nothing but a clumsy patchwork of conditions — you'll be able to tell at a glance what knowledge point any problem is testing.

**3. Independent Review**

Being able to follow the answer doesn't mean being able to solve the problem. Cover the answer and independently write out the problem's logical chain. Skip the actual computation — grasping the core logic is what matters. If you get stuck, that sticking point is your revision focus.

Repeat this process until you've fully resolved the sticking point.

**4. One Problem, Three Eats**

Don't do three problems — do one problem three times.

First pass: look at the answer and make sure you understand the logic. Second pass: solve it independently, produce the output yourself, and verify whether you truly understand. Third pass: modify the problem. Ask yourself — if this condition were removed, could this problem still be solved? If we changed "find A" to "find B," would the solution method change? When you can create variations of a problem, you are riding on top of the exam-setter's head.

## Four: Precision Output — Hit Exactly Where You Aim

### Extreme Score Orientation

Only with extreme score orientation can you achieve extreme output efficiency.

This is where the past exam papers you prepared earlier come in. Go to the library, set a timer, put your phone away, don't listen to music, don't take bathroom breaks midway — simulate the real exam environment as faithfully as possible. After finishing, grade yourself as rigorously as you can, and be honest with yourself.

The first practice paper rarely turns out well, and that's fine — the first paper is mainly for detecting gaps in your review. You can even be a bit brutal: if you're unsure about a question, just leave it blank.

During grading, note down your sticking points and common mistakes. If you don't understand something, just ask AI directly. AI is very intelligent and available on demand. Although AI occasionally hallucinates, that shouldn't stop you from using it boldly. Do everything you can to thoroughly digest this one practice paper. Don't move on to a new paper before you've fully absorbed it.

A few days later, find time to do the second practice paper. Follow the same process: simulate the exam environment as closely as possible, check whether previous issues have been resolved, and see if there are any new problems. If a mistake you've made before reappears, go back to where the mistake first happened and redo that specific question. Repeatedly digesting one problem is far more effective than skimming over a hundred.

The third practice paper should be done about three days before the exam. By this point, your review is nearly complete. Doing one more paper helps deepen your impressions, maintain your groove, and catch any final gaps.

At this stage, you should have a sheet of paper summarizing question-type strategies, common pitfalls, trap points, and more.

### The Cheat-Sheet Mindset

Disclaimer: what follows is purely a review technique — it is not an encouragement to cheat on exams.

If the professor suddenly allowed you to bring one A4 sheet of notes as a cheat sheet, what would you write on it?

Basic concepts? Formulas? Key and difficult points? Or problem-solving routines?

Whatever you write, to condense the content as much as possible, you must generalize at a high level, distill formulas, and summarize routines. This process itself forces you to produce output.

If it were me, I would also reference the A4 sheet I compiled from doing the past papers, and add in the traps I've fallen into — giving myself early warning about potential mistakes.

Once you've written these down, your brain naturally remembers them, and you won't need to bring the cheat sheet to the exam. So it's best to do this the night before the exam, then go straight to sleep afterward to reinforce memory.

The highest form of cheating is photocopying the textbook into your mind.

### The Grading Algorithm

Professors hate grading papers. They have no time to appreciate your literary flair. Their eyes work like scanners, hunting for scoring points. That said, professors will also try their best to match your answers against the standard answer key, doing what they can to help you pass — after all, failing a student is trouble for both sides.

Your answer sheet is written for the "human keyword-retrieval algorithm."

Approach your answers as if you're writing code, and optimize for this algorithm:

1. **Don't leave blanks.** The vast majority of professors will try to give you points wherever possible. Whether you know how to solve it or not, start by translating the problem conditions into mathematical symbols — for example, write "v=0" for "at rest." Doing just this often earns you 1–2 free points. If you write nothing, even a professor who wants to give you points has nothing to award.
2. **Answer in bullet points.** This applies to both humanities and STEM subjects. Present your answers line by line, as clearly as possible. This also makes grading easier for the professor.
3. **Put formulas first.** Write out the original formula before beginning the actual calculation. This way, the professor can see that you didn't just guess — you have a clear logical chain. As long as the formula is correct, you'll get most of the marks, even if the entire calculation is wrong. If you bury the formula inside the calculations so the professor can't follow your reasoning, you're on your own.

## Five: Exam-Day Control Engineering

### The -20 Rule

Energy always suffers some loss during transmission, and exam scores are no different.

Review is "input-side power," while the exam is "output-side power." Between them come losses: walking out of your dorm, arriving at the exam hall, seeing unfamiliar questions, sweaty palms, a blanking mind, calculator misclicks — these losses total roughly 20 points.

Aim for 100 in your review, and your actual score will typically be in the 80s. Aim for 80, and your final score will often be in the 60s — scraping by. Aim for 60, and your final score will usually be in the 40s — waiting for a retake or summer make-up.

If you want to pass, you must never settle for "just enough to pass." You must leave a safety margin for that 20-point loss.

In the first five minutes of the exam, your brain is in a foggy state. Don't rush into answering questions right away. After writing down your name and student ID, scan through the entire paper to familiarize yourself with the questions. Then, before you forget, immediately write down the formulas on scratch paper.

### Exam-Day Flow

You might be the type of person who reviews thoroughly but starts sweating and racing the moment you enter the exam hall, your mind going completely blank the second you see the paper, rushing through in a panic, only to regret everything the moment you hand it in.

I'm not entirely a clutch performer either. If you've met me in person, you'd know that I get very nervous during presentations. But for exams, I've developed a workflow that you might find useful:

1. **Change your bedsheets three days before the exam.** If it's been more than a month since you last changed them, it's time. Better bedding means better sleep. Sleep is the foundation of all mental energy. Don't delude yourself into sacrificing sleep for review — unless you only have three days to cram.
2. **The night before the exam, carefully review that sheet of paper** filled with strategies, common mistakes, and trap points. Also, mentally walk through what unexpected situations might arise the next day and write them down, so you're mentally prepared. Fear stems from the unknown. If you've already thought through many scenarios in advance, you won't panic as much when they actually happen.
3. **Bring some tissues.** Students with sweaty hands should pay special attention to this. Sweaty palms can't be dried just by wiping them on your clothes. Having some paper to wipe with, or to place under your hand on the paper, can make you feel more at ease.
4. **Stop reviewing 30 minutes before the exam.** If you've solidly followed all the previous steps, there's no need to keep reviewing — cramming last-minute only adds anxiety. Instead, close your eyes and meditate in your seat. Calm yourself and reduce mental clutter.
5. **Skip any question you can't figure out within two minutes.** Even though university final exam questions tend to follow relatively fixed patterns, there can still be variations. You can never predict whether the next question will stump you. Stubbornly wrestling with one question only increases panic — it's better to secure the points you can get first. Don't lose the forest for one tree.
6. **Keep your scratch paper neat.** If you finish early and want to go back and check your work, or if you're worried about mistakes, reviewing your scratch paper is a very effective method for catching potential calculation errors.
7. **Don't join post-exam discussions.** The moment you hand in your paper, nothing can be changed. Discussing it afterward is not only pointless but can easily disrupt your mindset. What if you compare answers and realize you got several wrong? Give up in despair? Comfort yourself? Finals aren't finished in a single day. Don't let one bad exam affect your performance on the rest.

## 6: The Ultimate Weapon: Agent-Based Interactive Learning

If you absorb the content above well, you can achieve a 3x efficiency boost. But the approach that follows can increase your efficiency by an order of magnitude, completely crushing traditional study methods.

The saying "the first 18 weeks tell you I'm in university, the last 2 weeks tell you why I got in" will no longer be a joke about last-minute cramming — it will become an obvious reality.

In the AI era, not using AI is a criminal waste of resources. But most people use AI only at the level of web chat: using AI to look up problems, or explain a concept.

And all they tell the AI is empty talk like "help me explain concept X," and what they get back is naturally as hollow and boring as an encyclopedia entry.

Any web-based AI has two limitations: 1) It can only solve one problem at a time and cannot solve an entire category of problems at once. 2) Large models have limited context capacity — once the conversation gets too long or too many files are uploaded, the intelligence degrades.

So this kind of interaction gets progressively worse over time. AI outputs become increasingly "brain-dead," missing key concepts, making calculation errors — these low-level mistakes become frequent.

Our approach is to use an agent: first give it an instruction manual, then let it read local files and generate Markdown documents, turning the conversation into node-based interactions that can also serve as prompts for the next round of dialogue. It also won't haphazardly reference files and memory, giving you inexplicable answers.

As for what teachers say — things like "even with AI, real thinking still has to be done by yourself" — treat these correct-but-useless platitudes as entertainment. They probably don't know how to use AI properly themselves.

### 1. Identity Definition

AI should not be "a smarter Baidu" — it should be a "senior collaborator."

AI is a probabilistic prediction model. It has ingested the entire internet's corpus, but it doesn't know whether you paid attention in class today, or what your course instructor's exam style is like.

If you don't tell it these things, it can only output a "least-likely-to-be-wrong" average — and the average means mediocrity and condescending lectures.

First, tell the AI "who you are." Only when you define its identity can it give you answers that hit the mark, rather than spouting grand but useless talk.

At the same time, in the first conversation, you must not only tell it "who you are" but also tell it "who I am" — for example, I only have 3 days to prepare for the exam, I know nothing about this subject.

Even if you say "I am an intellectually challenged PhD student," an absurd identity like that is still better than saying nothing at all.

Don't let the AI decide for itself what face to present to you. If you don't define it, it will brush you off with its most mediocre posture.

### 2. Context Supremacy

Even if you immediately tell the AI "who you are" and "who I am," its output will still miss the mark and fail to solve specific final exam problems.

AI possesses all the knowledge in the world, but it uniquely lacks this moment's context. It doesn't know your textbook edition, the difficulty of the lectures, or your teacher's exam style.

Garbage in, garbage out — this is an absolute truth in AI interaction.

It's like a PhD student who masters every subject but knows nothing about your situation — you need to feed it your background knowledge. So never start by telling it "summarize the key points of calculus for me." Instead, throw in all the textbooks you've used, the homework you've done, and the past exam papers you've worked through.

You can refer to the following directory structure:

```text
course-root/
  reference/
    textbook/
    ppt/
    photos/
    homework/
    past-papers/
    answer-keys/
    senior-notes/
  progress/
```

With these materials, the AI has context, can learn specifically for the knowledge points and exams you're facing, and its answers are far less likely to go off track.

When you tell it "I want to review Chapter 4," the AI won't give you content that seems important but won't actually be tested — instead, it will genuinely summarize the key exam points based on your files.

Putting reference materials into folders has another benefit: the AI won't be affected by "memory" functionality — it won't mix up subjects or remember strange things when it shouldn't.

Combined with the prompts above, the AI not only knows "who I am" but also knows "who you are." With this mutual understanding, the AI's answers hit even closer to the mark.

### 3. You Are the Master of AI

The week before the Gaokao, I happened upon a Tarot stall while walking along the riverbank. I had a reading done. I can't remember many of the details, but one line has stayed with me:

"You must grip the initiative tightly, just as the Empress grips her scepter."

AI can do the summarizing, deconstructing, and mock question generation for us — but the decision-making power in learning must remain in our hands. Letting AI take over completely might just lead you into a pit.

Driving can be delegated — but where to drive must be decided by us.

When we usually use AI to assist with solving problems, we tend to end with vague phrases like "what do you think?" or "help me break this down." Then the AI outputs a long process — maybe correct, maybe different from the reference answer — but one thing is clear: the key points aren't highlighted, and you don't know where to start.

Again, garbage in, garbage out. The input is an unclear instruction, so the AI, to be "safe," naturally won't highlight the key points in its output. It's afraid that what it highlights as "key" might not be what's on your exam — just like how you're afraid of answering wrong when suddenly called on in class.

Use clear, forceful commands to direct the AI, such as: "What are the 3 terms that must appear in this short-answer question?" or "Explain this theorem to me in terms a high school student would understand."

This way, the AI won't be afraid of outputting wrong — it can output boldly according to your requirements.

Always remember: you are the driver of this learning game; AI is merely your navigator and engine. Don't let the tool take the wheel and make strategic plans for you.

## 7: If You Only Have Three Days to Prepare

### You Only Have 24 Hours

You don't have 3 days — you only have 24 hours.

But the good news is: you don't need to learn the entire textbook; you only need to learn the entire exam paper.

Final exams are looming, and you haven't even started "pre-studying." You haven't read *Self-Study Science and Technology* either, so you decide to brute-force it and push yourself — 8 hours of study per day. Most speed-run courses on Bilibili are about 3–5 hours; let's take the average of 4 hours. Assume you still haven't grasped some parts after the first pass, so you listen a second time and summarize a rough outline from the textbook. The first day's 8 hours are gone just like that.

Day 2: you find past exam papers, or you have AI generate a mock exam for you. First attempt: 2 hours of simulated exam time passes, and the results are brutal — you start doubting whether you can even pass.

To avoid failing, you decide to thoroughly master the content that's relatively easy to score on. Simple fill-in-the-blank and multiple-choice questions gradually fall under your control, the earlier long-answer questions are conquered step by step, and the harder questions toward the end are strategically abandoned. 4 hours are put to use this way. And to be safe, you even memorize the answering format for long-form questions — so if your mind goes blank in the exam hall, you can at least scrape some partial credit. Another 2 hours go by.

Day 3: even after all the prep work, you're still panicking, feeling like there's really not enough time. But you still have 8 hours today — you can still salvage this. In the final stretch, you go over the outline and exam papers from the first two days repeatedly. Still uneasy, you transcribe a full set of answers to get a feel for the handwriting. The morning's 4 hours are used diligently. But this kind of review doesn't seem to calm your nerves — it only makes you more anxious. So you simply spend the afternoon's 4 hours playing games, and before bed, glance one last time at what you've prepared.

For passing, this is completely enough — completely, completely, completely enough.

Alright. The math is done. Now let's get into the practical steps.

### Just-In-Time

First, mindset: you only have 3 days, so don't expect a high score. Unless your teacher is exceptionally kind and really tries to pull you up, be mentally prepared for a score around 60–70%, just scraping by. For those academic beasts averaging 90+, a course at 60–70% might be a devastating blow — so I sincerely hope such students stop reading here.

Assuming 6 chapters per course, with 24 hours to allocate, that's about 4 hours per chapter on average. In those 4 hours, even trying to "understand" a difficult concept is a waste of time. In 3-day cramming mode, understanding is a luxury; memorization is a necessity.

Three days before the exam, "seeking deep understanding" is the fastest path to failing. Become an emotionless exam-solving machine — you can forget all this knowledge after the exam, but that's a problem for after the exam.

Our principle: don't study what won't be tested, rote-memorize what's hard to understand, and give up on low-value problems.

First, prepare the textbook and PPT slides — but don't read them cover to cover. Use them for checking the table of contents and for reference. Spend 3 hours going through the textbook and slides to quickly summarize a knowledge outline.

Then find past exam papers, and mark every tested point on this outline. The areas with the densest marks are your review priorities. As for the blank areas, strategically abandon them.

If you want to use AI for summarization, put the textbook or slides into a folder and tell it: "I only have 2 hours to review this chapter. Please ignore all background knowledge and derivations, and tell me directly: 1. What are the 3 must-know formulas here? 2. When encountering this question type, what is usually the first step in the standard solving procedure?"

If there's something you don't understand, just memorize it. Give up on "why" and focus only on "what." When you see "find the limit," immediately invoke L'Hôpital's rule; when you see "extreme value," instantly react with derivative equals zero.

Cut and paste similar question types from the past 3 years' exams together and look at 10 of them in a row. You'll find they're all the same — only the numbers change.

Memorize the shape of the solving procedure — don't ask why. Even if you can't fully solve it in the exam hall, writing down the overall procedure will get you most of the points.

Decisively give up the hardest 20% of the finale-level questions, and spend your time on the 80% of foundational questions instead. It's better to score full marks on easy questions than zero on hard ones.
